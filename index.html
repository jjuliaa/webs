Computational Evolution of Sensing & Cognition

Life self-organizes and evolves on the basis of molecular and information based dynamics. Natural environments are high-dimensional and contain potentially infinite kinds of useful signals, and yet the sensory systems of organisms each become attuned to highly specifically relevant ranges of signals that they efficiently represent. Developmental processes produce chemical gradients that can store and provide positional information across the cells of the embryo. Both of these processes also evolve and change over the lifetime and over generations of experience, where different molecular mechanisms may serve similar informational purposes.

I study what might be shared across different information dynamics from molecules to minds, to understand how different kinds of complex cognition evolve. Are there more primitive “building blocks” of complex cognition that are repurposed by evolutionary processes and if so, how do evolutionary dynamics recombine these building blocks to produce different kinds of complex sensing and cognition? Or, are similar information dynamics in different organisms and at different scales computationally and fundamentally different? If so, how do we characterize these differences, especially in ways that could be informative for both biological and artificial kinds of cognition.

While I study this I also hold a philosophical question about the conceptual frame of information theory and computation as applied to life. This more broadly includes computational modeling as a scientific episteme. Are these the right ways to think about what biological life is and does? Are models at best, metaphors? Are we limited by our notions of computer based computation, or can we rethink what biology does in its similarity and differences? Is it even possible to have a unified understanding of the logic of life given the ever evasive messiness of biology? While slower, I find it highly productive to be engaged in interdisciplinary thinking, with both biological research and critical science theory and philosophy. My work is at the intersection of Computational Cognitive Neuroscience, Evolutionary Biology, Unconventional Computing and Science and Technology Studies.
