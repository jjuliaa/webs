About Research

Life self-organizes and evolves not only through molecular substrates but information-based ones. The natural environments in which life arises are diverse and vast, containing many potentially usefully regular signals. Through evolution, development and learning, organisms become attuned to highly specific, biologically meaningful subsets of those signals and develop sensory systems and cognitive processes that represent and act on them. Cognition is the study of how living things can detect information (sensing and perception), encode or store information (memory), compress, structure and generalize from information (causal inference).

I study the evolution of computational cognition. Are there more primitive “building blocks” of the computations underlying different cognitive processes? How do evolutionary dynamics recombine these building blocks to produce different kinds sensing and cognition? Or are similar information-processing capacities in different organisms and at different scales realized through fundamentally different computational mechanisms? If so, how do we characterize these differences, especially in ways that could differentiate computations across biological and artificial systems (such as in their architectures, internal representations, capacities and limits, energy-information tradeoffs, processing dynamics).

My goals are to contribute to empirical methods and conceptual frameworks that allow us to characterize computation in different systems. I don’t assume that all living information processing is the same or that all biological and artificial systems are convergent and can be described by a single computational theory. Rather, I want to test whether information-processing principles recur across living systems, identify the conditions under which they arise, and characterize differences across substrates and scales.

Alongside this work, I love investigating epistemic questions about biology and computational modeling: when different models produce the same outputs, what makes one a deeper explanation of biological organization? Can models really help us arrive at deeper explanatory insights into the logics of biology? My current dissertation examines how living systems evade the models used to characterize them and how what models miss might itself become a source of knowledge. These questions motivate comparing models and living systems under perturbation and developing situation specific null models. This is necessarily interdisciplinary and brings together computational cognitive neuroscience, evolutionary biology, unconventional computing, and Science and Technology Studies.

About me

I am an interdisciplinary researcher in the bay area, CA, studying how living systems evolve to sense, learn, and process information across scales. My approach combines computational modeling, comparative biology, and critical inquiry into what scientific models can and cannot explain.

My training includes a SMArchS in Design and Computation at MIT and work in cognitive science and artificial intelligence through MIT CSAIL. I also completed a master’s degree in Psychology working in the Krubitzer lab for Evolutionary Neuroscience, where I compiled a dataset mammalian brain regions involved in sensory processing to analyze how these areas vary across species. This background allows me to think in interdisciplinary ways, connecting computational, biological, and evolutionary phenomena.

I am currently pursuing a PhD at the University of California, Davis. My doctoral work brings these perspectives together through research and simulation of the computational transition from unicellular (multinucleate) to multicellular organization in the slime mold Physarum polycephalum, alongside work in Critical Simulation Studies examining what biological models represent, omit, or distort. I also work on computational models of how sensory information and memory may feedback, and how this could contribute to the evolution of kinds of memory.

I have an enduring fondness for fungi and slime molds, and the unusual evolutionary strategies of parasitic plants and parasitoid wasps. They will probably find their way onto this site too. :)
